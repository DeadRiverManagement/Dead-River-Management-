Dimensional weight, or DIM weight, is a pricing method where carriers charge based on a package's size instead of its actual weight when the box is large and light. UPS and FedEx bill you for the greater of actual weight or dimensional weight, so a bulky, near-empty box can cost far more than the scale suggests.

If you ship anything light in a large box, dimensional weight is probably one of the biggest hidden line items on your invoice. Here is how it works and how to stop overpaying for shipping air.

## How is dimensional weight calculated?

Dimensional weight is calculated by multiplying a package's length, width, and height, then dividing by the carrier's DIM divisor. For UPS and FedEx domestic shipments the divisor is 139. You are billed on whichever is greater, the actual weight or the dimensional weight.

The formula:

**(Length x Width x Height) ÷ 139 = dimensional weight in pounds**

Example: a box measuring 20 x 18 x 12 inches is 4,320 cubic inches. Divided by 139, that is about 31 pounds of dimensional weight. If the actual contents weigh 8 pounds, you are still billed as if the package weighed 31 pounds. You pay for the empty space.

Both carriers publish the rule in their service guides: see the [UPS guide to dimensional weight](https://www.ups.com/us/en/support/shipping-support/shipping-dimensions-weight.page) and the [FedEx explanation of dimensional weight](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/dimensional-weight.html).

## What is the DIM divisor 139?

The DIM divisor is the number carriers divide cubic size by to get dimensional weight. A lower divisor means higher billable weight. UPS and FedEx use 139 for most domestic shipments, so a smaller divisor on some accounts makes DIM charges even steeper.

The divisor is the lever carriers control. When they lower it, your billable weight rises without any change to what you actually ship. That is why divisor changes buried in annual rate updates can raise costs quietly. Some negotiated contracts carry different divisors, which is one of the first things a rate review checks.

## Why does dimensional weight inflate my shipping bill?

Dimensional weight inflates your bill because you are charged for a package's size, not its contents. Oversized boxes, excess void fill, and low-density products all trigger dimensional-weight billing, so you pay for space rather than goods.

Three culprits drive most DIM overcharges:

- **Boxes too big for the product**, leaving empty space you pay to ship.
- **Excess void fill** that lets you use a larger box than needed.
- **Low-density items** (pillows, apparel, plastics, footwear boxes) where size always beats weight.

Carriers also round each dimension and the final weight up, which nudges billable weight higher than a quick calculation suggests. Measure your boxes the way the carrier does, at the longest point on each side, before you trust an estimate.

## How can I reduce dimensional weight charges?

You can cut dimensional-weight charges by right-sizing packaging to your product, reducing void fill, using carrier-appropriate box dimensions, and auditing invoices to catch DIM billing errors and negotiate a better divisor.

Practical steps:

1. **Right-size your boxes** so packaging matches product size. Two or three box sizes chosen around your best sellers usually beat one "fits everything" carton.
2. **Cut void fill** and use boxes that reduce empty volume.
3. **Standardize on dimensions** that keep billable weight low across your common products.
4. **Audit and negotiate.** An invoice audit catches DIM billing errors, and a stronger [small package agreement](/#services) can improve the terms you ship under.
