Yes, late deliveries are refundable — on services the carrier still guarantees, and only if you ask. If a covered shipment is delivered after its committed time, UPS and FedEx will refund or credit the transportation charges. Neither carrier does this automatically. The shipper has to identify the failure and file the claim.

That gap between "refundable" and "refunded" is where the money sits. Carriers publish the guarantee, honour it when claimed, and are under no obligation to volunteer that they owe you anything.

## Which FedEx services are covered by the money-back guarantee?

As of the FedEx Service Guide effective February 12, 2026, the money-back guarantee applies to a specific list of express services. FedEx states plainly that the guarantee for all other FedEx services **remains suspended until further notice.**

The currently covered services, per the [FedEx Service Guide](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/service-guide/money-back-guarantee.html):

**US domestic**

- FedEx First Overnight
- FedEx Priority Overnight
- FedEx Standard Overnight
- FedEx 2Day A.M.
- FedEx First, Priority and Standard Overnight Extra Hours (contract services)

**US import and export**

- FedEx International First
- FedEx International Priority
- FedEx International Priority Express
- FedEx International Priority Freight
- FedEx International Priority DirectDistribution and DirectDistribution Freight

This list is narrower than most published guidance suggests, and the history is why. FedEx suspended the guarantee on December 1, 2025, reinstated it for the domestic services above on January 13, 2026, and for the international services on February 12, 2026. A great deal of advice still circulating online describes a broader guarantee that covered FedEx Ground and Home Delivery. Check the service guide before assuming a shipment is covered — this list has changed more than once and can change again.

FedEx also excludes shipments delayed by regulatory changes from refund eligibility, alongside the usual force-majeure exclusions.

## Does UPS still guarantee delivery times?

UPS operates a service guarantee on defined services, with the same basic structure: if a covered shipment misses its commitment time, UPS refunds or credits the transportation charges on request. Coverage varies by service and has been revised repeatedly, so the current [UPS Service Guarantee terms](https://www.ups.com/us/en/support/shipping-support/legal-terms-conditions/service-guarantee.page) are the only reliable reference for which of your services qualify today.

Two things hold regardless of which services are on the list:

- **The refund is request-only.** UPS does not credit late shipments unless a claim is filed.
- **The claim window is 15 days.** Late claims are refused, and there is no appeal on timing.

Do not take a service's coverage on faith because it was covered last year. Verify against the current terms, then build your claim process around whatever is actually guaranteed.

## How long do you have to claim a late delivery refund?

You have 15 days. Both carriers work on a 15-day claim window, and both enforce it strictly. A shipment delivered a day late in week one is worthless by week four.

This deadline is the whole reason refund recovery is an operational process rather than an accounting exercise. A monthly invoice review is already too slow for a meaningful share of the claims in it. By the time a month-end close surfaces a late delivery from the first week of the period, the window has closed on it.

The practical consequence: claims have to be identified and filed weekly, against live carrier data, or they are not filed at all. This is the same cadence a [parcel audit](/blog/what-is-a-parcel-audit/) runs on, and for the same reason.

## Why do most late delivery refunds go unclaimed?

Most refunds go unclaimed because claiming them properly is a weekly reconciliation job that no shipping team has time for. It means matching every guaranteed shipment against its commitment time, filing each claim individually inside 15 days, and then confirming the credit actually appeared on a later invoice.

The work breaks into four steps, and each one loses people:

1. **Identify.** Pull tracking scans for every guaranteed shipment and compare the delivery timestamp against the commitment time. Not the delivery date — the time.
2. **Qualify.** Strip out shipments excluded by weather, force majeure, address problems or a service that is not currently guaranteed.
3. **File.** Submit each qualifying claim separately, with evidence, inside the window.
4. **Reconcile.** Match approved credits back to the claims that generated them. A filed claim is not money. A confirmed credit is.

Step 4 is the one that is skipped most often, and it is where the leakage hides. Claims get denied, or approved and never credited, and nobody notices because nobody is holding the list.

## What else qualifies for a refund besides late delivery?

Late delivery is the most familiar refundable event, but it is not the largest category on most invoices. Billing errors usually are.

Alongside service failures, the recoverable categories include:

- **Duplicate charges** — the same tracking number billed across two invoice periods
- **Dimensional-weight errors** — wrong measurement or wrong divisor applied, covered in [dimensional weight explained](/blog/dimensional-weight/)
- **Residential surcharges** applied to commercial addresses
- **Address-correction fees** on addresses that were correct as tendered
- **Manifested but never shipped** — labels created and billed, then voided or never tendered
- **Contract rate not applied** — billed at published rates on a service your agreement covers

These have no money-back-guarantee mechanism behind them. They are simply invoice errors, and they are recovered by disputing the charge with evidence. Together they typically outweigh late-delivery refunds, which is why refund recovery and invoice auditing belong in the same process rather than being run separately.
